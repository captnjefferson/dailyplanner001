## 2. The person read

Maggie Winters grew up in North Arlington and studied design at the Corcoran, where her senior thesis was a hand-sewn zine on how badly sex-education materials are designed. At 23, "pink-haired," she staged the first free Pop Wedding outdoors in January 2014. Pop! Wed Co., a pop-up elopement business she built with Steven Gaudaen while working full time at the DC digital agency ISL, drew NPR and the Washingtonian and ran seven years. She closed it in December 2021 with a public eulogy that names the cause plainly: "this amazing little company has been limited by one thing — me." In January 2020, at 29, she and Zach Goodwin, a colleague from her previous agency (ISL, by inference), started January Third with five people, two months before the pandemic; after January 6 they renamed it This January in 2023. She is proud of the pitch deck they built on a train before their first client meeting, of growing the team "from five people to twenty," of the 2026 Inc. 5000 and of NOOK, the artist residency she founded this year. She writes openly about her own fertility care, and that writing is where her women's-health lane comes from.

What she cares about, in her own words: saying taboo things plainly ("Simply saying the word 'vagina' in an ad is a radical idea," 2021); women's health and sex education, a line from her thesis to the "Interests" on her new site; craft through iteration ("It's not magic. It's the 53rd try"); independents over holding companies, where you get "a senior pitch followed by a junior team" (2024); candor ("Let's start telling each other the whys behind our nos," 2022); and DC as "the next great creative city." She praises other shops' craft (BUCK "can make anything shine") and clients who trust the team. She mocks "blands," twenty-person meetings, spec pitches, decks of tiny charts and the "perfect romance" script.

INFERENCE: she builds tools for her own problems, then wonders whether they are products. Pop! Wed was "designed around our life"; the agency's hiring software came out of 800 applications in a Google Sheet (Inc., 2026); and a "fun-but-failed" uterus pinball machine for sex education became an iOS game (2019). INFERENCE: since spring 2026 she has been widening her lane beyond the agency: her rebuilt site (September) invites people to "borrow her brainpower" for women's-health boards and guest lectures, and her May post on building with Claude closes with an open call for collaborators. INFERENCE: agency growth runs through the two founders. A "VP of New Business" seat was listed on the team page from December 2023 to April 2026 and then removed, and the new marketing coordinator works "closely with our CCOs" so that "our best moments don't quietly happen in a vacuum."

How she talks: exclamatory and self-mocking; her bio calls her a "human exclamation point." Short declaratives, numbered lists, parentheses that undercut her own point, all-caps openers ("NO PREAMBLE STRAIGHT INTO IT!!!!!!!"), swearing in personal posts, emoji (✨🤓👀), and a closing ask for feedback ("How could I have made this article better? What's missing?"). She signs "Love, Maggie." Words she owns: weird, fun, tiny, adventure, rad, "exceptional weirdos," "KPIs > awards." On cold email she was blunt in 2022: "If you haven't started a conversation, in my mind you have no obligation to respond."

Where she shows up: Shorty Awards jury (2026); Chief; the AAF DC board (2023 Form 990; not on today's roster); WBJ 40 Under 40 (2025); talks at DC Design Week, Advertising Week DC, Adweek DC, Refresh DC, Adobe Creative Jam and AdWorld (mostly undated); The Creative Department happy hours (2022–26), Pecha Kucha (2025), Art All Night (2026); podcasts STORY (2018), Reel II Real (2020) and Brands & Campaigns (2024); bylines in Muse, Medium, LBB; quoted in Inc. (August 2026).

What she consumes: on Substack, Lenny's Newsletter, Product Fridays, METHOD®, A SHORT LIST (an agency-matchmaker letter), Slow Boring, Ctrl Alt Right Delete and WelcomeStack; on LinkedIn [LN], Fast Company's AI Decoded, the Inc. 5000 letter, Clios, Ad Age and Adweek. Frameworks she borrows: Kenn Adams's Story Spine, Byron Sharp, Peter Levitan. Tools she names: Claude and Claude Cowork, Figma, Netlify, Keynote ("our love language") and, at the agency, Midjourney.
