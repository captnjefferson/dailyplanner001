## 2. The person read

Ryan Brickwood is a plant-finance man who came to public accounting late and still talks like the plant. He attended Kentucky Wesleyan, played Division 1 center field at Akron (a May 2002 Akron box score lists him in center), and took his accounting degree and MBA at Akron. He spent about 17 years at J.M. Smucker, "mostly on the manufacturing side": plant controller at the Memphis jam, jelly and ice-cream-toppings plant, then regional plant finance, sales finance and away-from-home finance. In 2022 a former Smucker colleague who had moved to Rea called about leading the manufacturing practice; he met the team and it "just felt like home." Colleagues describe what came next: the role was "largely undefined" and "he went out and created it," new to public accounting, and within about a year he won the firm's innovation award. What he is proud of, in his telling, is work on other people's floors: the client that doubled revenue with flat profit until an item-and-customer cost study fixed its pricing; the line cut from 100 SKUs to 75 that earned more on the same revenue; a monthly close pulled from 15–20 days to five. He has told no failure story in public. His creed covers it: "Fall forward... Mistakes will happen but learn from them and continue to grind."

He returns to five things. Know your real cost before you touch price: without the data, how can you ask for an increase? People, process and technology, in that order, or "it'll eventually break." Decide on facts: "I need all the facts... You'll sleep better at night. At least I do." The owner's company is family: "This is your baby... You got your kids in there." And outwork everyone: his early letters to Plain-community owners closed on "Outwork your competitors and be relentless" and Mandela's "rising every time we fall." He praises owners who want to grow and teams that "take it and run with it." He needles complacent clients, companies that switch cost methods every year ("I'm not joking. Some companies do that"), jargon, and buying more system than you need: "I'm not going to order a Cadillac if they need a Honda."

INFERENCE: he is building the practice in his own image and using Manufacturing Education Day as its front door. The managers around him are former plant controllers and CFOs. A client testimonial traces its relationship to "one of their Manufacturing Days." The day grew from one city to three in two years, and in September his stage widened to a supply-chain panel at ACG Columbus. INFERENCE: AI is a subject he carries as practice leader more than one he lives in. In a 48-minute interview he named it once, in a list with dashboards. His own LinkedIn mentions it only in event promotion. The polished AI lines under his byline read like the firm's content operation.

In speech he is fast, warm and long; one colleague said that if you meet Ryan and "don't get your ear talked off," that would be a surprise. He says "you know" constantly and opens answers with "Great question." He reaches for sports ("blocking and tackling," "bet on that horse," crawl-walk-run), uses plant-floor examples with dollar figures, and owns up to a "long-winded answer." His own written voice is short letters that close on a borrowed quote. Since late 2025 the letters read as edited, and the quotes are gone.

He shows up on Rea's own stages (Manufacturing Education Day, the March 2025 Dublin forum), on a vendor podcast (Koble Systems' Taking Stock, July 2025), at ACG Columbus (September 2026), and on the cover letter of Plain & Simple, a quarterly print newsletter Rea built for Amish and Mennonite business owners. He has read Good to Great "a couple times," recommends Traction/EOS, reads the Wall Street Journal "at times," and tells owners to join executive roundtables. His younger brother's tribute sets the register: NBA jerseys, hat on backwards, "you're just one of us."
