## 2. The person read

Jason McMurray is a construction insurance broker whose whole public record sits inside one firm. In December 2024 he wrote that he had been at Holmes Murphy "nearly nine years," which puts his start around 2016; nothing public says where he worked or studied before. He works on the property and casualty side (the insurance on what a contractor builds, drives and gets sued over), and his specialty is group captives: insurance companies that a group of contractors own together. In early 2026 he introduced himself on camera as "a client executive on the property casualty team with a focus on captive business." By June 2026 he was a vice president and one of 27 employees the firm made shareholders that year. The one hard number he has put on his own work: on April 1, 2025, "my clients renewed $14.8 million in Property Casualty premiums with Holmes Murphy — all within the Construction sector," general contractors, heavy highway operations and commercial electricians "from the West and East coasts to the Midwest and Southeast." Family, health, faith and community: **not found** on any public page an agent can open; his LinkedIn is the lead's to read.

He returns to four things. **Control:** insurance is a contractor's "second largest expense behind payroll," and "the control over it seems to be diminishing" (2023); the captive answer is to "go from renting your insurance to truly owning it" (2026). **Integrity in selling:** "Having the integrity to say no is not a lack of competitiveness," and "we never place a contractor into a captive simply because it's available or advantageous for us." **Knowing early:** "How early do you want to know there's a problem?" and "Hope isn't a forecasting strategy, but data is." **Peers:** a captive "can serve as a peer group that promotes improvement," and long-term members "say that the greatest value isn't the financial return—it's that they've become better businesses." He praises contractors who back field instinct with data, advisors who are honest "when the answer wasn't easy," and owners who study their wins as hard as their losses. He needles forecast meetings that turn into "blame sessions," optimism passed off as a forecast ("optimism doesn't change the math"), and advisors quick to say "yes."
