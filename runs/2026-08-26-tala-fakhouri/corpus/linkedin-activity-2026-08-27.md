### [6] TIRS paper (2mo, 156/9/16) — her flagship substantive post
"Hot off the press! New in Therapeutic Innovation & Regulatory Science, open access: 'Real-World Evidence for Drug Approvals: Moving Toward Greater Regulatory Clarity.' A decade into the 21st Century Cures Act, real-world evidence still rarely makes it into drug approvals. The usual explanation is data quality. Data quality is critical. But, we argue the more stubborn constraint is operational: even fit-for-use RWD gets stuck on three unresolved policy questions.
1. Data standards. FDA's submission requirements are built around CDISC, designed for protocol-driven trials. Forcing heterogeneous RWD into SDTM and ADaM is costly and can obscure provenance. FHIR helps at the data-exchange layer but is not a regulatory-grade submission format on its own.
2. Data access and inspection. It is still unclear when sponsors must submit patient-level versus aggregated data, and how FDA inspects source records. The December 2025 device guidance signals a shift, but its implications for drugs and biologics are undefined. Federated and hybrid architectures, already proven in Sentinel and DARWIN EU, deserve a serious look.
3. Human subject protections. Applying 21 CFR Parts 50 and 56 to non-interventional studies creates real ambiguity. We propose distinguishing three categories of non-interventional designs by risk profile, and clarifying where consent and IRB review actually apply.
For each barrier we offer concrete steps FDA can take now. Thank you to the amazing co-authors Rasika Kalamegham (Genentech/Roche), Nicole Mahoney (Novartis), Rose Purcell (Takeda), and Mike D'Ambrosio (Parexel). Read it here: [lnkd.in link]. Comments and pushback welcome."

Years ago, I worked on Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's flagship health survey NHANES, the national survey that runs mobile examination units across the country to collect health data. NHANES data is used to evaluate health policies and to inform a future policies. Standing in front of Eli Lilly and Company Mobile Research Units on the Plaza this afternoon (see pic) I was struck by how familiar the operational challenges felt, and reminded of how genuinely powerful these mobile units are when they're done well. Bringing the clinic to the community is how we make clinical research a care option and these units are one way to reach rural communities and hard to reach populations. When trials struggle to enroll, the easy story is that patients are skeptical or risk-averse. But Dr. Janelle Sabo laid out the actual top three reasons, and none of them are about fear:
1. Location. Americans spend 80% of their lives within 25 minutes of home. In rural areas, it's 50 (let me know if I got this number wrong!). If a trial isn't where someone lives, works, or picks up their kids, it's a logistics problem that is hard to overcome.
2. Nobody asked them. The average doctor visit is 7 minutes and doctors simply don't have time to explain clinical research to participants. Dr. Monica Webb Hooper from NIH made the same point: the myth that underrepresented communities don't want to participate falls apart the moment you actually ask.
