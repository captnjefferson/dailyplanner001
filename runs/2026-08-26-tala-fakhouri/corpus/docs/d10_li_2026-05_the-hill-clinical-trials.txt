Spent the afternoon at The Hill's Reimagining Clinical Trials event in DC, and walked away with a really wonderful flashback. Years ago, I worked on Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's flagship health survey NHANES, the national survey that runs mobile examination units across the country to collect health data. NHANES data is used to evaluate health policies and to inform a future policies. Standing in front of Eli Lilly and Company Mobile Research Units on the Plaza this afternoon I was struck by how familiar the operational challenges felt, and reminded of how genuinely powerful these mobile units are when they're done well. Bringing the clinic to the community is how we make clinical research a care option and these units are one way to reach rural communities and hard to reach populations. When trials struggle to enroll, the easy story is that patients are skeptical or risk-averse. But Dr. Janelle Sabo laid out the actual top three reasons, and none of them are about fear:
1. Location. Americans spend 80% of their lives within 25 minutes of home. In rural areas, it's 50. If a trial isn't where someone lives, works, or picks up their kids, it's a logistics problem that is hard to overcome.
2. Nobody asked them. The average doctor visit is 7 minutes and doctors simply don't have time to explain clinical research to participants. Dr. Monica Webb Hooper from NIH made the same point: the myth that underrepresented communities don't want to participate falls apart the moment you actually ask.
3. Money. Lost wages, mileage, childcare. The US is one of the few countries that taxes participant stipends.
